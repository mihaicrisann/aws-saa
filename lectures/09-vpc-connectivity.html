Connecting VPCs and data centres

Peering, Transit Gateway, Site-to-Site VPN, Direct Connect, VPC endpoints and PrivateLink — and the exact cue that selects each one.

Six ways to connect a VPC to something else. Each has an unmistakable cue in the question, so this lecture is mostly about learning the cue rather than the protocol.

Peering and its three limits

A one-to-one private link between two VPCs, in any Region and any account. Traffic uses private IPs and never touches the internet. Then the limits — each one is an exam answer:

  1. Not transitive. A↔B and B↔C does not give A↔C. You need a third peering, and n VPCs need n(n−1)/2 connections. This is exactly the pain Transit Gateway solves.
  2. CIDRs must not overlap. No exceptions, no NAT workaround.
  3. Route tables must be updated on both sides, and security groups can reference peer security groups only within the same Region.

Transit Gateway

A Regional hub. Every VPC, VPN and Direct Connect gateway attaches once, and route tables on the TGW decide who can reach whom. It supports transitive routing, IP multicast, and cross-Region peering between Transit Gateways.

Its route tables also let you build segmentation — e.g. every spoke can reach a shared-services VPC, but spokes cannot reach each other. "Isolate environments while sharing central services" is a TGW answer.

Three phrasings, three answers

"Encrypted connection quickly / temporarily" → Site-to-Site VPN. "Consistent low latency, high throughput, mission critical" → Direct Connect. "Direct Connect, but it must be highly available / must not be a single point of failure" → two Direct Connect connections at different locations, or one DX with a VPN as backup — the cheaper answer, and usually the intended one.

Components you should recognise: a Virtual Private Gateway (VGW) attaches a VPN/DX to a single VPC; a Direct Connect Gateway lets one DX reach VPCs in multiple Regions; private VIF reaches VPC private IPs, public VIF reaches public AWS endpoints (S3, DynamoDB) over the DX; a Customer Gateway represents your on-prem device. VPN gives two tunnels for redundancy — use BGP for dynamic failover.

VPC endpoints

By default, an EC2 instance calling s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com goes out to a public endpoint — via the IGW or NAT. Endpoints keep that traffic on the AWS network.

A frequent trap

"On-premises servers must reach S3 privately over Direct Connect." A gateway endpoint cannot do this — it is only a route inside the VPC. The answers are an interface endpoint for S3, or a public VIF on the Direct Connect. Meanwhile, for instances inside the VPC, always prefer the free gateway endpoint.

PrivateLink generalises this: you put your service behind an NLB, publish it as an endpoint service, and consumers create interface endpoints in their VPCs. No peering, no route table changes, no CIDR overlap problem, and traffic is one-directional (consumer → provider). "Expose an internal service to hundreds of customer VPCs" → PrivateLink, not peering.

Also worth knowing

AWS Resource Access Manager (RAM) shares subnets, Transit Gateways, and Route 53 rules across accounts in an Organization. The shared VPC pattern — one network account owns the VPC and shares subnets with app accounts — is how large organisations avoid VPC sprawl.

Decision list

Which connection?
Two or three VPCs, simple, cheap→VPC Peering
Dozens of VPCs, or a mesh that's become unmanageable→Transit Gateway
Overlapping CIDRs, or exposing a service to many consumers→PrivateLink
On-prem, needed this week, encrypted→Site-to-Site VPN
On-prem, consistent latency, sustained high volume→Direct Connect (+ VPN backup for HA)
Private access to S3/DynamoDB from inside the VPC→Gateway endpoint (free)
Private access to any other AWS service, or from on-prem→Interface endpoint

Check yourself

25 VPCs across 4 accounts all need to talk to a shared services VPC, and to on-premises.
Transit Gateway, shared across accounts with RAM, with the Site-to-Site VPN or Direct Connect attached to the same TGW. A peering mesh would need 300 connections and still wouldn't be transitive.
A SaaS vendor must expose their API to your VPC, but their CIDR overlaps yours.
PrivateLink — an interface endpoint in your VPC talks to their NLB-fronted endpoint service. Overlapping CIDRs are irrelevant because nothing is routed between the networks.
Direct Connect is installed and traffic is flowing. The auditor says data in transit must be encrypted.
Direct Connect is private but not encrypted. Run a Site-to-Site VPN over the Direct Connect (or enable MACsec where supported).
Private-subnet instances must reach S3 without a NAT Gateway, at zero extra cost.
A Gateway VPC endpoint for S3, with its prefix-list route added to the private route tables and, optionally, an endpoint policy restricting which buckets.

Take away

  1. Peering is non-transitive and forbids overlapping CIDRs; scale past a few VPCs with Transit Gateway.
  2. VPN = fast to build, internet-dependent, encrypted. Direct Connect = consistent, private, slow to provision, unencrypted by default.
  3. Highly available Direct Connect = a second DX at another location, or a VPN as backup.
  4. Gateway endpoints: S3 and DynamoDB only, free, route-based, not reachable from on-prem.
  5. Interface endpoints/PrivateLink: an ENI in your subnet, works for nearly everything, reachable over DX/VPN, chargeable.
  6. PrivateLink is the answer whenever CIDRs overlap or you're publishing a service to many consumers.
