The exam playbook

How the questions are built, the keyword-to-service map, elimination tactics, timing, and what to do in the last ten minutes.

Design Secure Architectures Design Resilient Architectures Design High-Performing Architectures Design Cost-Optimized Architectures Strategy Keywords Elimination Timing

You now know the material. This lecture is about the other half of the score: how the questions are built, how to dismantle them, and how to spend the 130 minutes.

The format, precisely

  • 65 questions, 130 minutes — exactly 2 minutes each. Multiple choice (one correct of exactly four) or multiple response (two or more correct of five or more; the stem always tells you how many).
  • Only 50 are scored. 15 are unscored pretest items scattered invisibly through the paper. A question that feels bizarre may simply not count — don't let it rattle you.
  • Scaled score 100–1000, pass at 720. It is not a percentage; there is no per-domain pass requirement.
  • Domain weights: Secure 30%, Resilient 26%, High-Performing 24%, Cost-Optimised 20%.
  • No penalty for wrong answers. Never leave a question blank.

How a question is constructed

Almost every question has the same anatomy:

[ Scenario         ]  A company runs a three-tier web app on EC2 …
[ Complication     ]  … traffic is unpredictable and the database is
                      overwhelmed during sales events …
[ The actual ask   ]  Which solution meets these requirements
[ The qualifier    ]  … with the LEAST operational overhead?

The qualifier is the answer key. Several options usually work technically; the qualifier picks between them. Read it first and last.

QualifierIt is selecting for
MOST cost-effectiveServerless, Spot, colder storage tiers, gateway endpoints, scaling to zero
LEAST operational overhead / "fully managed"Managed services over self-managed: Lambda over EC2, Fargate over EC2, Aurora over self-hosted MySQL, Firehose over custom consumers
MOST secureLeast privilege, roles not keys, private subnets, encryption, VPC endpoints, no public access
HIGHEST availability / "no manual intervention"Multi-AZ, automatic failover, more redundancy — cost is not the concern here
MINIMAL downtime (migration)DMS with ongoing replication, blue/green, read replica promotion
MINIMAL changes to the applicationLift-and-shift-friendly: EFS instead of rewriting to S3, Amazon MQ instead of SQS, RDS instead of DynamoDB
REAL-TIME vs NEAR real-timeKinesis Data Streams vs Firehose. The word "near" is doing real work.

Elimination tactics

Aim to remove two options instantly, then reason about the remaining two. Options are usually wrong for one of these reasons:

  1. Technically impossible. Attaching an EBS volume across AZs. A CNAME at the zone apex. An LSI added to an existing table. A gateway endpoint reached from on-premises. Lambda running for 30 minutes. ACM certificates on EC2.
  2. Solves a different problem. Read replicas offered for high availability. Multi-AZ offered for read scaling. CloudFront offered for a UDP workload.
  3. Violates the qualifier. It works, but it's the expensive option in a cost question, or the hand-rolled option in a "least operational overhead" question.
  4. Contains an anti-pattern. Hard-coded access keys. Opening 0.0.0.0/0 on port 22. Storing session state on instances. Public database subnets. Root user for daily work. Any option containing an anti-pattern is wrong even if the rest of it is right.
Answer-shape tells

Extreme words ("always", "never", "all traffic") are usually wrong. Options that require writing and maintaining custom code lose to a managed service when the qualifier mentions overhead. If two options are nearly identical apart from one word, that word is the entire question — find it and decide on it. And if two options are both wrong for the same reason, the answer is one of the other two.

Timing

  • Two minutes per question is the budget. Track it at milestones: you should be near question 25 at the 50-minute mark and question 50 at 100 minutes.
  • Three passes. Pass 1: answer everything you're confident about within 90 seconds, flag anything else, and always select something before moving on. Pass 2: work the flagged questions with the time saved. Pass 3: only revisit answers where you can articulate a concrete reason to change — first instincts are right more often than not.
  • Long scenarios often hinge on one sentence. Read the final question sentence first, then the scenario, and you'll read it looking for the right thing.
  • The exam is not adaptive. A hard run of questions means nothing about your score.

The highest-leverage habit

Log every wrong answer with the reason you got it wrong — knowledge gap, misread the qualifier, or fell for a distractor. Re-read that log before every session. Reviewing your own mistakes beats doing new questions by a wide margin, because the same handful of confusions cause most losses: Multi-AZ vs read replicas, gateway vs interface endpoints, SQS vs Kinesis, pilot light vs warm standby, latency vs geolocation routing, CloudWatch vs CloudTrail vs Config.

The day itself

  • Online proctored: clear desk, one monitor, good light, check in 30 minutes early. Test centre: arrive early and bring two forms of ID.
  • Nothing new the day before. Skim your mistake log, the keyword map above, and the takeaways at the end of each lecture.
  • You get the pass/fail on screen at the end and the scaled score by email within a few days.
  • If it goes wrong: 14-day wait, and you keep the mistake log — which by then is the most valuable study asset you own.

Take away

  1. 65 questions, 50 scored, 130 minutes, pass at 720/1000. No penalty for guessing — answer everything.
  2. Find the qualifier (cheapest / least overhead / most secure / most available) before choosing. It decides between options that all technically work.
  3. Eliminate on impossibility, wrong-problem, qualifier violation, and anti-patterns.
  4. Anti-patterns are automatic disqualifiers: hard-coded keys, 0.0.0.0/0 on 22, session state on instances, public databases.
  5. Two minutes per question, three passes, flag rather than stall, and never change an answer without a reason you can state.
  6. Keep a mistake log and review it more than you do new questions.
